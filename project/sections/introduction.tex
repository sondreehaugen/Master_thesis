\section{Background for the choice of the topic}
\label{sec:background}

% WHY THIS MATTERS
In 2024, Norway registered 124 fatalities and serious injuries in 
intersection accidents, a nearly 30\% increase compared to the average of the previous four years and the highest level since 2015 
\citep{trafikksikkerhetsutviklingen2024}. Intersections are among the 
most demanding traffic environments, where motor vehicles, cyclists, 
and pedestrians interact within limited space, creating numerous 
potential conflict points. 

In current practice, traffic data at intersections is commonly collected through manual counts, which are short-term, periodic observations typically lasting 1--4 hours and limited to peak periods \citep{SVV_V714}. This approach is labor-intensive and captures only a snapshot of traffic conditions, making it poorly suited to characterizing temporal variation across times of day, days of the week, or seasons.


Norway has pursued a systematic approach to reducing road traffic fatalities over several decades, with annual deaths falling from approximately 500 in the 1970s to 87 in 2024 \citep{trafikksikkerhetsutviklingen2024, regjeringen2024_meldst14}. This progress is closely linked to Vision Zero, anchored in the National Transport Plan from 2001, which aims to eliminate all road traffic fatalities by 2050 with interim targets for 2030 \citep{regjeringen2024_meldst14}. However, as shown in \autoref{fig:drept_hardt_skadde}, recent trends suggest Norway is not on track to meet these targets.

AI-based methods are increasingly recognized as potential decision-support tools in transport applications, in line with the National Transport Plan’s emphasis on data-driven and proactive traffic safety management. However, the extent to which such technologies can deliver traffic data of sufficient accuracy and reliability under real operating conditions remains insufficiently explored. The following section, therefore, identifies key areas requiring further investigation.

\section{Knowledge Gap}
Computer vision (CV) has become an increasingly important component of intelligent transportation systems (ITS). Comprehensive survey studies show that vision-based methods are applied across a wide range of transportation-related tasks and play a growing role in improving traffic monitoring, safety, and operational efficiency \citep{CVSurvey}.

Previous studies demonstrate that deep learning–based object detection models, particularly YOLO-based architectures, can achieve high accuracy and near real-time performance for vehicle detection and counting when evaluated on established benchmark datasets or in constrained traffic environments \citep{Everingham10,HuggingFacePascalVOC, Song2019VisionBasedVehicle, smartcities6050134, YOLO-PEL}.

However, the literature also identifies limitations that motivate the research questions in this thesis. \citet{Maity2022LastDecadeVehicleDetection} highlight that many AVD studies rely on a limited set of vehicle classes, motivating \textbf{RQ1} on fine-grained vehicle classification. The same survey further notes that robustness is often not explicitly evaluated under occlusion and dense traffic, which, together with reported challenges in adverse lighting and weather and in small-object/multi-scale detection \citep{DeepLearningTrafficScene2025,YOLO-PEL}, motivates \textbf{RQ2} on performance under varying operational conditions. Finally, calls for practical validation in real-world deployments \citep{YOLO-PEL} and the need for more robust, end-to-end tracking pipelines for complex scenarios \citep{Hassan2024} motivate \textbf{RQ3}, which concerns sustained autonomous operation and reliability over time.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq}
%This thesis aims to evaluate how YOLO-based object detection and tracking systems can be designed and configured to extract accurate and reliable traffic parameters from video data at real-world intersections, and to what extent such systems can operate autonomously over time without manual intervention. Three research questions are sought to be answered in this thesis.

This thesis evaluates how YOLO-based detection and tracking systems can be configured to extract accurate traffic parameters from intersection video, including fine-grained vehicle classification and turning movement counts. Existing systems are typically validated on limited vehicle classes and short evaluation periods, leaving open questions about performance under varying real-world conditions and sustained autonomous operation across dynamic lighting transitions. Three research questions are addressed to fill these gaps.

  
    \textbf{RQ1: How accurately can YOLO-based detection models extract fine-grained vehicle classification at an intersection?} 
    
    \textbf{RQ2: How do different model configurations (detector + tracker) perform in extracting turning movement counts under varying lighting conditions?}
    
    \textbf{RQ3: To what extent can a vision-based traffic monitoring system operate autonomously over time without manual intervention while maintaining reliable performance?}

\section{Scope and limitations}
% WHAT WE WON´T COVER

\subsection*{Scope}
This study is delimited as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geographic:} Analysis of one T-intersection in Norway.

    \item \textbf{Model scope:} Evaluation of YOLO26, the most recent YOLO release, fine-tuned for traffic object detection.

    \item \textbf{Parameters:} Focus on traffic volumes, vehicle composition, turning movements, and the presence of pedestrians.
    
   \item \textbf{Temporal:} Video data collected during February--April 2026, capturing variations across time of day and weather conditions.
    
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Limitations}

The study has the following limitations:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Camera and privacy constraints:} The traffic camera was not positioned or configured for optimal object detection. Certain areas of the video are intentionally blurred to comply with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) requirements, which may affect detection performance near privacy-masked regions. 
    
    \item \textbf{Environmental conditions:} Detection performance may be affected by weather (rain, snow, fog) and lighting conditions (night, shadows).
    
    \item \textbf{Intersection type:} The pipeline is limited to a T-intersection; findings may not generalize, as different geometry or camera configurations are unknown.

    \item \textbf{Vulnerable road users:} Low instance counts of cyclists and pedestrians in the dataset limit the reliability of performance estimates for these classes.
    
    
\end{itemize}


\section{Structure of The Thesis}
% HOW THE THESIS IS ORGANIZED

The thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} presents the motivation and background for autonomous traffic monitoring. Chapter~\ref{chap:theory} reviews existing traffic count methods, object detection theory, and relevant literature on YOLO architectures and multi-object tracking. Chapter~\ref{chap:methods} describes the dataset development, fine-tuning of YOLO26 models for fine-grained classification, and implementation of a dual-configuration tracking pipeline using BoT-SORT and ByteTrack. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents detection and tracking performance across varying lighting conditions. Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses the results and addresses the research questions in relation to prior work, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes contributions and directions for future research. Supplementary material, including implementation code and raw results, is provided in the appendices.
