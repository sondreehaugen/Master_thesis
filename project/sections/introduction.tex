\section{Background for the choice of the topic}
\label{sec:background}

% WHY THIS MATTERS
In 2024, Norway registered 124 fatalities and serious injuries in 
intersection accidents, a nearly 30\% increase compared to the average of the previous four years and the highest level since 2015 
\citep{trafikksikkerhetsutviklingen2024}. Intersections are among the 
most demanding traffic environments, where motor vehicles, cyclists, 
and pedestrians interact within limited space, creating numerous 
potential conflict points.

In current practice, traffic data at intersections is often collected using manual traffic counts. According to the Norwegian Public Roads Administration’s \textit{Traffic Data Guide}, manual registrations are typically conducted as short-term, periodic counts with a normal duration of 1-4 hours, often limited to peak periods \citep{SVV_V714}. Such counts are labour intensive and provide only a snapshot of traffic conditions, which makes it difficult to capture temporal variations across days, weeks, or seasons. This results in a gap between the data quality required for robust intersection design and the methods commonly used to obtain traffic data \citep{SVV_V714}. The handbook notes that intersection registrations remain an exception, as reliable automated methods for this application are not yet available \citep{SVV_V714}.
% zero vision
Norway's Zero Vision, anchored in the National Transport Plan in 2001, aims to eliminate all road traffic fatalities by 2050, with interim targets of maximum 350 fatalities and seriously injured by 2030, of which no more than 50 are fatalities \citep{regjeringen2024_meldst14}. With intersection accidents reaching their highest level since 2015, achieving these targets requires improved understanding of traffic patterns and more targeted safety interventions.

Today, 80\% of the Norwegian population lives in urban areas, and continued urbanization is a central premise of national transport policy \citep{regjeringen2024_meldst14, ssb_centralisation}. Norway’s zero-growth objective requires that all growth in urban passenger transport be absorbed by public transport, cycling, and walking \citep{regjeringen2024_meldst14}. This increases pressure on urban intersections, which must accommodate more vulnerable road users while maintaining safety. However, pedestrians and cyclists face five and ten times higher risk per kilometre than car occupants, respectively \citep{svv_tiltaksplan_trafikksikkerhet_2026_2029}.

The economic implications are substantial: the Value of a Statistical Life (VSL) is estimated at 46.7 million NOK (2023), while serious injuries carry an average cost of 16.8 million NOK \citep{svv_v712_2025}. Given the complex interactions between heavy vehicles and vulnerable road users in intersections, ensuring adequate design and dimensioning depends on high-quality traffic data capturing vehicle composition, turning movements, and temporal variation.

Norway's long-term transport policy, outlined in the National Transport Plan 2025–2036 (NTP) \citep{regjeringen2024_meldst14}, identifies digitalization and new technology as key enablers for achieving more efficient and sustainable transport systems. The NTP emphasizes exploiting existing transport data to manage and influence travel patterns, while prioritizing cost-effective measures across wider geographical areas. 

AI-based methods are recognized as potential decision-support tools for applications such as intersection design, in line with the plan’s emphasis on data-driven and proactive traffic safety management. However, the extent to which such technologies can deliver traffic data of sufficient accuracy and reliability under real Norwegian operating conditions remains insufficiently explored. The following research gap section therefore identifies key areas requiring further investigation.

\section{Knowledge Gap}
Computer vision (CV) has become an increasingly important component of intelligent transportation systems (ITS). Comprehensive survey studies show that vision-based methods are applied across a wide range of transportation-related tasks and play a growing role in improving traffic monitoring, safety, and operational efficiency play a growing role in improving traffic monitoring, safety, and operational efficiency \cite{CVSurvey}.

Previous studies demonstrate that deep learning–based object detection models, particularly YOLO-based architectures, can achieve high accuracy and near real-time performance for vehicle detection and counting when evaluated on established benchmark datasets or in constrained traffic environments \citep{Everingham10,HuggingFacePascalVOC, Song2019VisionBasedVehicle, smartcities6050134, YOLO-PEL}. However, much of the existing literature formulates vehicle detection as a generic object detection problem, often using a limited number of vehicle classes and datasets consisting of still images or highway scenarios. Moreover, prior work rarely evaluates whether the extracted outputs correspond to traffic parameters required for intersection design, such as turning movements and vehicle composition, nor whether detection performance remains robust under real-world conditions including occlusions, dense traffic, and adverse lighting or weather. Consequently, there is limited empirical evidence on whether deep learning–based object detection applied to fixed roadside cameras can generate traffic data suitable for intersection design purposes.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Questions}
% HOW WE´LL APPROACH IT
The objective of this thesis is to evaluate to what extent deep learning–based object detection applied to traffic camera data can provide traffic parameters of sufficient accuracy and reliability to serve as decision-support input for the design and dimensioning of road intersections in accordance with Norwegian design guidelines. Based on this problem statement, two research qestiono are sought to be answered in thes thesis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Which traffic parameters defined in N100 and V121 can be extracted from traffic camera data using deep learning–based object detection?
    \item How accurately can deep learning–based object detection models estimate traffic volumes, vehicle composition, and turning movements compared to manual traffic counts?
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and limitations}
% WHAT WE WON´T COVER

\subsection*{Scope}
This study is delimited as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geographic:} Analysis of one urban intersection in Norway, representative of typical at-grade intersections in urban environments.
    
   \item \textbf{Temporal:} Video data collected during February--March 2026, capturing variations across time of day, weather conditions, weekdays, and weekends.
    
    \item \textbf{Model scope:} Evaluation of multiple YOLO models (YOLOv8n, YOLOv8s, YOLOv8m) pre-trained on COCO dataset and fine-tuned for traffic object detection.

    
    \item \textbf{Parameter:} Focus on traffic volumes, vehicle composition, turning movements, and presence of pedestrians/cyclists—key parameters for intersection design and safety assessment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Limitations}

The study has the following limitations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environmental conditions:} Detection performance may be affected by weather (rain, snow, fog) and lighting conditions (night, shadows).

    \item \textbf{Camera quality:} The positioning and image quality of the traffic camera may not be optimal for object detection. Certain areas in the video are blurred or obscured due to GDPR compliance requirements.
    
    \item \textbf{Experimental focus:} The study demonstrates feasibility and validation against manual counts but does not address operational deployment, system integration, or GDPR compliance.
    
    \item \textbf{Intersection type:} Limited to at-grade intersections; findings may not generalize to roundabouts, grade-separated interchanges, or rural intersections.
    
    \item \textbf{Behavioral analysis:} Focuses on detection, counting, and turning movements.
\end{itemize}
