\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter synthesizes the findings from Chapter~\ref{chap:results} and evaluates them against the
overall research aim and the three research questions in
Section~\ref{sec:rq}. The discussion links detection and tracking performance to the theoretical framework in Chapter~\ref{chap:theory}, justifies model and tracker selection, and compares the observed results with relevant prior work. It further examines daytime and nighttime behavior and live deployment performance.
Finally, the chapter critically addresses methodological and statistical limitations and
outlines the scope of valid conclusions.


\section{YOLO26 Performance}
This section summarizes the still-image detection performance of the YOLO models, covering training/validation behavior, independent test results, key error modes, and comparison with prior work.
\subsection{Training and Validation}
The training and validation curves for both YOLO26n and YOLO26m (\autoref{sec:yolo_train_val}) were characterized by a rapid performance increase in the first 10--15 epochs, followed by a plateau around epoch 55. This behavior is consistent with the design goals of YOLO26’s training innovations described in Section~\autoref{sec:yolo26-advancements}. Progressive Loss Balancing (ProgLoss) and the MuSGD optimizer are intended to promote more stable and efficient convergence by dynamically adjusting loss component weighting and improving optimization behavior \citep{sapkota2026yolo26keyarchitecturalenhancements}. In this study, the early stabilization suggests that both models quickly learned the dominant visual features present in the custom dataset. After the plateau, YOLO26n triggered early stopping, while YOLO26m completed the full 100 epochs with limited additional gains.

\subsection{Testing}
On the independent test set of 193 images containing 761 object instances, both models demonstrated solid overall performance, with YOLO26m outperforming its smaller counterpart (mAP$^{50}$ = 0.808 vs.\ 0.693; mAP$^{50-95}$ = 0.638 vs.\ 0.556). The results confirm that the models are particularly effective at detecting the dominant ``Car'' class and the expanded heavy-vehicle categories, which is one of the main objectives of this study. However, performance was weaker for the bicycle and person classes. In addition to being less represented in the dataset, these vulnerable road users appear much smaller in the SVV camera view than larger vehicles. With the available stream resolution and camera distance, a cyclist or pedestrian may be represented by only a small number of pixels, reducing the amount of visual information the model can use to separate them reliably from background clutter and nearby objects. This makes detections more sensitive to occlusions and image artifacts, and contributes to lower accuracy compared with the larger and more visually distinctive vehicle classes.

Bus detection also underperformed relative to the other heavy-vehicle categories on the independent test set. While this can partly be attributed to the lower number of bus instances in the dataset, it is still notable because \textit{bus} is included as a standard COCO category, meaning that the pre-trained YOLO26 weights already provide prior knowledge for this class. At the same time, the validation metrics show the opposite pattern, where \textit{Bus} performed best among the heavy-vehicle classes. This discrepancy suggests that bus performance is sensitive to the composition of the random splits and the limited number of bus examples, rather than reflecting a consistent limitation of the detector.

Overall, the results suggest progress toward the gap noted by \citet{Maity2022LastDecadeVehicleDetection} on limited class coverage in AVD studies, as we extend the label set and achieve strong performance for most added categories; nevertheless, conclusions for very rare classes (e.g., bicycles) remain uncertain due to limited test instances.

\subsubsection{Precision-Recall Inconsistencies}
Although YOLO26m consistently achieves a higher mAP than YOLO26n on both the validation and test sets, the relative differences in precision versus recall vary between the two splits. In our case, the dataset comprises images recorded at two different sites and across multiple times of day and weather conditions. Since the train/validation/test partitioning was performed randomly at the image level, the resulting splits are not guaranteed to be perfectly matched in terms of scene composition (e.g., illumination, traffic density, occlusion frequency and class composition). Moreover, the reported "All" precision and recall (\autoref{tab:test_results_n}/\ref{tab:test_results_m}) are macro-averaged (unweighted) across classes, meaning each class contributes equally regardless of how many instances it contains.

Consequently, rare classes and edge cases can noticeably influence aggregate metrics: for example, in the YOLO26n validation set (\autoref{tab:val_results_n}) the \textit{Bicycle} class has precision (=1.0) and recall (=0.0) because the model missed all three bicycle instances (0 TP, 3 FN) while producing no bicycle predictions, making precision undefined and reported as 1.0 by convention. As one of eight classes, this contributes $1/8$ to the reported ``all'' precision. Taken together, the observed validation-test deviations are therefore most plausibly explained by sampling variability in the random splits combined with macro-averaging, rather than inconsistent model behavior.

\subsubsection{Limitations of the mAP Metric}
While mean Average Precision (mAP) is the standard benchmark for object detection, it shares the same unweighted macro-averaging property: AP is computed per class and then averaged so that each category contributes equally, independent of frequency (\autoref{eq:mAP_calc}). This structure can bias the overall score in imbalanced datasets, where rare classes may have unstable AP estimates. To illustrate this sensitivity, \autoref{tab:mAP_no_bicycle} also reports mAP recalculated without the \textit{Bicycle} class. This is not intended to exclude bicycles from the evaluation; rather, it quantifies how much the overall mAP is reduced when a class with negligible test support (\(n=2\)) is included. Given this scarcity, the low \textit{Bicycle} AP likely reflects limited training exposure and sampling noise rather than a stable estimate of general performance.

\subsection{Qualitative Error Analysis}
As shown in the confusion matrices in \autoref{sec:test_results}, a substantial share of the errors are associated with the implicit \textit{background} category, i.e., annotated objects that are not detected (false negatives) and predicted objects without a corresponding annotation (false positives). Qualitative inspection indicates that these background-related errors primarily arise from three recurring error modes.

First, \textbf{false negatives} occur under degraded visibility and low-contrast conditions (e.g., fog/night), where objects become difficult to distinguish from the surroundings (\autoref{fig:FN_fog}). Second, \textbf{false positives} appear in visually complex scenes with occlusions and clutter, particularly for small or distant objects, where partial object boundaries can trigger extra detections (\autoref{fig:FP_heavy_occlusions}). These observations align with the research gaps discussed in Section~\ref{sec:research_gap}: \citet{DeepLearningTrafficScene2025} emphasize that YOLO-based detectors still require further improvements in small-object detection and robustness under adverse lighting conditions, and \citet{YOLO-PEL} similarly stress the importance of practical validation in real-world deployment scenarios, which frequently include challenging illumination.
Third, although YOLO26 is designed as an end-to-end, NMS-free detector, occasional \textbf{duplicate predictions} (multiple highly overlapping boxes on the same object) were observed (\autoref{fig:triple_prediction}); only one box can match the ground truth, while the remaining boxes are counted as false positives and therefore reduce precision.

It is also important to note that some apparent background errors can be influenced by annotation policy and human oversight (e.g., distant objects excluded from labeling or missed annotations), particularly in scenes affected by privacy masking and long sight distances. Additional examples of FNs and FPs are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:qea}. 

Importantly, these per-frame detection errors do not translate one-to-one into traffic-counting errors in the complete pipeline. Many of the false negatives and false positives discussed above were observed in footage from the Kjørbekk station, which is excluded from the deployed pipeline due to the quality limitations discussed in \autoref{sec:discussion_limitations}. For the operational setting, detections are integrated over time using multi-object tracking, so the same road user is observed across multiple frames, providing repeated opportunities for correct detection and class stabilization. As a result, intermittent missed detections and short-lived false positives are partly mitigated in the trajectory-based origin–destination counting.


\subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}
Contextualizing these results against published benchmarks reveals the contribution of this study. \cite{YOLO-PEL} introduced YOLOv8-PEL as a lightweight variant of YOLOv8n, achieving 0.669 mAP$^{50}$ with 2.23 M parameters on the COCO-Vehicle dataset. In direct comparison, the YOLO26n model (approximately 2.4 M parameters, 0.693 mAP$^{50}$) and YOLO26m (approximately 20.4 M parameters, 0.808 mAP$^{50}$) demonstrate competitive or superior accuracy despite comparable or slightly higher parameter counts. Notably, our task presented higher inherent difficulty: while the COCO-Vehicle benchmark grouped all heavy vehicles into a single category, we expanded the truck class into three distinct sub-types, requiring the model to learn finer visual distinctions.

The mAP$^{50}$ of YOLO26n already is numerically higher than the 66.9 mAP$^{50}$ baseline from \cite{YOLO-PEL} by approximately 2.4 percentage points on a more challenging multi-class task, while the 0.808 mAP$^{50}$ of YOLO26m represents a substantial further improvement. At the same time, the comparison should be interpreted cautiously: our dataset is substantially smaller (1282 frames) than typical COCO-derived benchmarks (often tens of thousands of images), and the task domain is narrower (intersection-focused vehicle detection rather than general COCO-Vehicle scenes).
Accordingly, the main contribution is not only competitive absolute accuracy, but also evidence that domain-specific fine-tuning of modern architectures can deliver operational performance in specialized settings with modest data volumes.


\section{Tracking Performance}

The fine-tuned YOLO26 models were integrated into the complete tracking pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:method_tracking_od}. This section evaluates how detection quality, tracker selection, and environmental conditions (daytime vs.\ nighttime) collectively affect vehicle trajectory reconstruction and origin--destination counting.

\subsection{Overall Performance Drivers}

A central finding across all experiments is that tracking performance is primarily constrained by detection quality rather than tracker selection. This is consistent with \citet{Hassan2024}, who emphasize that detection forms the foundation of multi-object tracking, as missed detections directly remove the information required to maintain object identities across frames.

Across the 15-minute daytime configuration experiments, YOLO26m consistently outperforms YOLO26n regardless of tracker choice, achieving class errors between 0.8--2.5\% and route errors between 0.4--1.0\%, compared to 2.9--5.5\% class error and 17.8--18.6\% route error for YOLO26n. This indicates that the smaller model becomes a bottleneck in complex scenarios involving occlusions and dense traffic. 

In contrast, differences between trackers are comparatively small when paired with YOLO26m. All three tracker configurations achieve identical total counts (0.0\% total error) in the 15-minute test, suggesting that once detection quality is sufficient, different tracking algorithms produce similar aggregate outcomes. Tracker choice instead affects how errors are distributed across individual routes and classes rather than the overall count.

\subsection{Daytime Performance}

Daytime conditions represent the most demanding operational scenario due to high traffic density, frequent occlusions, and complex interactions between vehicles. 

In the 15-minute configuration experiment, the custom Day BoT-SORT setup combined with YOLO26m achieved the best overall performance, with class, route, and total errors of 0.8\%, 0.4\%, and 0.0\%, respectively, as well as perfect pedestrian counting. This confirms that the configuration provides a strong balance between detection sensitivity and identity consistency under stable lighting conditions.

The improved performance can be attributed to the parameter configuration of the tracker. Lower values for \texttt{track\_high\_thresh} and \texttt{new\_track\_thresh} increase recall by allowing more detections to initiate and maintain tracks, which is particularly beneficial for partially occluded or small vehicles. At the same time, a relatively high \texttt{match\_thresh} preserves strict association once tracks are established, reducing identity switches. Disabling global motion compensation further improves stability in this setting, as the camera is static and motion compensation may introduce unnecessary noise. Enabling ReID also contributes positively in daylight, where strong visual features support reliable appearance-based matching.

When evaluated on the 1-hour rush-hour video, the same configuration maintains strong performance, achieving a 0.5\% total error (1072 predicted vs.\ 1067 ground truth), with 2.3\% cell error and 6.2\% route error. Although slightly worse than the 15-minute test, this result reflects the increased difficulty of sustained dense traffic conditions. In practical terms, an overcount of +5 vehicles over one hour is negligible for most traffic engineering applications.

At the route level, performance is strongest for the dominant flows (S$\to$N and N$\to$S), with errors of 2.2\% and 1.2\%, respectively. These directions also experience the highest traffic volumes, suggesting that the system is robust under continuous high-load conditions. Larger deviations occur on low-volume routes, such as S$\to$E, where a +4 vehicle error corresponds to a 22.2\% relative deviation. This illustrates a general property of traffic evaluation: small absolute errors can translate into large percentage errors for rare movements.

Class-level analysis shows that car classification is highly reliable (0.9\% error), even under dense traffic. Misclassification is primarily observed among the heavy vehicle subclasses (light truck, heavy truck, semi-trailer), which share overlapping visual features, particularly under occlusion or challenging viewing angles. Some of the observed discrepancies (e.g., +3 light trucks and -3 heavy trucks) may also reflect annotation uncertainty rather than purely model error. Importantly, these class-level confusions have limited impact on overall traffic counts, which remain highly accurate.

Performance for motorcycles and bicycles remains weak. In the evaluated sequence, detected motorcycles corresponded to bicycles upon manual inspection, reflecting both the limited representation of these classes in the training data and their small visual footprint in the camera view. This reinforces that the current system is optimized for motorized vehicles rather than vulnerable road users.

From a practical perspective, ByteTrack remains the most computationally efficient configuration, offering higher runtime performance. However, it is consistently outperformed in accuracy by BoT-SORT-based configurations. This highlights a clear trade-off: ByteTrack may be preferable in real-time or resource-constrained deployments, whereas the custom Day BoT-SORT configuration is better suited for high-accuracy traffic analysis.

\subsection{Night-Time Performance}

Nighttime conditions represent a fundamentally different operating regime compared to daytime, characterized by reduced visibility, strong artificial illumination, and lower traffic volumes. While fewer vehicles simplify the tracking problem in terms of scene density, the visual quality of detections is significantly degraded due to headlight glare, reflections, and motion blur. As a result, the relative importance of tracker configuration increases, as the system must compensate for noisier and less reliable detections.

Despite these challenges, YOLO26m continues to outperform YOLO26n overall, although the performance gap between the models is smaller than in daytime conditions. Notably, YOLO26n combined with BoT-SORT Conservative achieves the lowest class error (8.2\%), indicating that the smaller model can remain competitive in classification under low-light conditions. However, YOLO26m consistently achieves lower route error and the lowest total error, making YOLO26m + BoT-SORT Conservative the best-performing configuration overall (Table~\ref{tab:od_nighttime_all}). This suggests that, under degraded visual conditions, robust detection combined with stable tracking is more critical for accurate turning movement estimation than marginal differences in class-level performance.

The improved performance of the conservative BoT-SORT configuration can be attributed to its emphasis on tracking stability and false positive suppression. In nighttime conditions, where detections are inherently noisier, this trade-off becomes particularly important. The elevated \texttt{match\_thresh} (0.90) and \texttt{proximity\_thresh} (0.75) enforce strict association criteria, reducing identity switches when visually similar vehicles are in close proximity. This is especially relevant at intersections, where vehicles frequently queue, overlap, and execute turning maneuvers that can temporarily obscure distinguishing features.

The extended \texttt{track\_buffer} (90 frames) improves robustness by allowing tracks to persist through short-term occlusions common at night, preventing fragmented trajectories and inflated counts. Moderately elevated detection thresholds further reduce spurious tracks by suppressing low-confidence detections typical in low-light conditions.

Disabling global motion compensation is appropriate given the static camera setup, while deactivating ReID reflects the short temporal duration of vehicle trajectories in intersection scenarios. Under these conditions, strict geometric and appearance-based matching is sufficient to maintain identity continuity without introducing additional model complexity.

A key challenge in nighttime detection is the effect of headlights and reflections on wet road surfaces. These conditions can cause large parts of a vehicle to become overexposed, effectively removing texture and color information and making different vehicle types appear visually similar. In some cases, vehicles are reduced to bright, saturated regions with limited structural detail, which significantly reduces the discriminative information available to the detector. This directly impacts both detection reliability and class separability, contributing to missed detections and systematic class confusion.

This effect is particularly evident in the classification of heavy vehicles. The YOLO26m model performs best in four out of six evaluated classes (car: 0.8\%, light truck: 0.0\%, bus: 0.0\%, motorcycle: 50.0\%), but shows large relative errors for heavy truck (250\%) and semi-trailer (71.4\%). A closer inspection reveals that these errors stem primarily from systematic confusion between the two classes (+4 heavy trucks and -4 semi-trailers), rather than random misclassification. Under nighttime conditions, the distinction between these categories becomes especially difficult, as identifying features such as trailer length and axle configuration are often obscured by motion blur and lighting effects. As a result, even manual annotation becomes uncertain, indicating that part of the observed error may reflect ambiguity in the ground truth rather than purely model limitations.

These class-level ambiguities also propagate into route-level errors. Although the total number of vehicles per direction remains correct, misclassification between heavy vehicle subclasses leads to an elevated route error (11.8\%). This highlights an important distinction between evaluation metrics: while total counts remain robust across configurations, fine-grained movement analysis becomes more sensitive to classification uncertainty under challenging lighting conditions.

Motorcycle detection presents an additional challenge. Due to their small size, high speed, and limited visibility under low-light conditions, motorcycles are difficult to detect reliably, even during manual review. In many cases, they appear only briefly and with minimal visual detail, resulting in both missed detections and incorrect classifications. This reinforces that performance differences between configurations are less pronounced for such classes, as all models struggle with the same fundamental visual limitations.

Turning movements further increase the complexity of nighttime tracking. In particular, vehicles traveling from east to south introduce challenges due to the combination of turning motion, changing orientation, and temporary occlusion. As vehicles rotate and move across the field of view, their appearance can change significantly between frames, making it difficult for the tracker to maintain consistent identity. This can lead to fragmented tracks or incorrect associations, even when detections are present.

Despite these challenges, the overall performance of the system in nighttime conditions remains strong. Given the difficulty of manually interpreting the scenes, especially under glare and motion blur, the low total error demonstrates that the pipeline is capable of extracting reliable traffic counts under adverse conditions. It is also important to consider that manual ground truth annotation is inherently more uncertain at night, particularly for small or fast-moving objects. As such, part of the observed discrepancy between prediction and ground truth may reflect human uncertainty rather than purely model limitations.

Overall, the results show that lighting conditions significantly influence both detection and tracking performance. However, appropriate configuration of the tracking module can effectively compensate for degraded visual input, maintaining stable trajectory reconstruction and accurate turning movement estimation across varying illumination conditions

\subsection{Adaptive Dual-Tracker System}

The adaptive dual-tracker system represents the final stage of the pipeline and the practical pathway toward continuous 24/7 deployment. The system monitors HSV saturation levels in real time and dynamically switches between day-mode (YOLO26m + Day BoT-SORT) and night-mode (YOLO26m + Night BoT-SORT Conservative) configurations. This design enables automatic adaptation to changing illumination conditions without manual intervention, while maintaining configuration-specific performance benefits.

The dual-tracker validation on the 10-minute twilight segment demonstrated perfect accuracy across all routes and vehicle classes. While this result is encouraging, the primary contribution of this experiment is not the absolute accuracy on a short sequence, as both daytime and nighttime performances have already been established in dedicated evaluations.

Instead, the key objective of this experiment is to validate system behavior under non-stationary conditions, where illumination changes during runtime. In such scenarios, static configurations are insufficient, as performance depends on the alignment between environmental conditions and tracker parameters. The adaptive switching mechanism ensures that the system continuously operates under near-optimal settings, even as visual conditions transition between regimes.

In this sense, the dual-tracker system demonstrates that the pipeline can maintain stable tracking performance and consistent movement-based outputs without manual reconfiguration. This represents an important step toward fully autonomous operation in real-world traffic monitoring, where lighting conditions vary continuously over time.

This aligns with the research gap discussed by \citet{Hassan2024}, who argues that future work should explore deep learning-based tracking with an emphasis on more end-to-end MOT solutions to improve robustness and accuracy in complex real-world scenarios.

\subsection{Tracking Challenges}

This subsection summarizes the principal recurring challenges encountered in the tracking experiments, their practical impact on the pipeline, and the mitigation strategies applied or recommended for future work.

\subsubsection{Zone-boundary sensitivity}

The gate-zone counting logic was developed iteratively. An initial line-crossing approach was tested, but it proved fragile in practice: if a track ID was temporarily lost due to occlusion, missed detections, or short classification dropouts exactly at the counting line, the vehicle was not counted. This motivated a transition to polygon-based gate zones, which substantially reduced sensitivity to single-frame failures. With zone-based logic, vehicles can still be counted when identities are re-associated after short interruptions (e.g., via ReID).

A second issue was zone geometry. Vehicles traveling from E$\to$S sometimes followed trajectories that briefly entered the north area (E$\to$N$\to$S in image coordinates), which caused route ambiguity. To address this, the north zone was resized to better separate trajectories; however, this introduced a trade-off, as an overly small north zone increased the risk of missing true north-bound vehicles. Therefore, the counting rule was revised to use the \emph{first stable zone} as origin and the \emph{last stable zone} as the destination, combined with a dwell-time constraint. This reduced spurious transitions near boundaries and improved route assignment robustness under occlusion and dense traffic.

\subsubsection{Dense traffic}

Dense traffic is a major tracking challenge because close vehicle spacing and frequent overlaps increase both missed detections and identity switches, particularly during rush-hour queues (e.g., when pedestrian crossings temporarily stop traffic). In these situations, multiple vehicles of similar appearance may occupy a small image region, which makes association more ambiguous and raises the probability of ID swaps and short track fragmentation.

To reduce this effect, the pipeline was tuned toward identity continuity and recall (permissive track creation, appearance matching, and post-processing of fragmented tracks). A practical prioritization was also applied: maintaining accurate \emph{total} counts and route-level flows was considered more important than strict fine-grained class purity during the most congested periods. This trade-off is reflected in the 1-hour daytime evaluation, where aggregate counting remained strong (+0.5\% total error; 1072 predicted vs.\ 1067 ground truth), while some residual class-level confusion persisted in dense scenes.


\subsubsection{Occlusion}
Occlusion remains a primary source of error in dense traffic scenarios. When vehicles overlap or are obscured by larger objects, the system faces a heightened risk of missed detections and identity switches. While the tracker is designed to manage brief interruptions, prolonged occlusions often lead to fragmented trajectories.

To mitigate this, a post-processing occlusion recovery mechanism was implemented. Empirical results demonstrate the efficacy of this approach: the algorithm successfully identified and resolved 19 distinct occlusion events during the one-hour day segment. By rectifying these events, the system directly addresses a critical gap in current Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) literature. Specifically, this implementation moves toward the future research directions proposed by \cite{Hassan2024}, who emphasize that robust MOT solutions must prioritize occlusion handling to reduce ID switches.

Class confusion among heavy vehicle subclasses stems from their inherent visual similarity, which is further compounded by occlusion and challenging viewing angles. While this affects class-level accuracy, its impact on total vehicle counts and route-level estimates remains limited.

Overall, detection quality emerges as the primary limiting factor in the pipeline. Addressing occlusion robustness  and class discrimination would therefore likely yield the greatest gains in end-to-end tracking performance.

\subsubsection{Lighting variability}

Lighting conditions are a persistent source of tracking uncertainty. Day--night transitions, headlight glare, wet-road reflections, shadows, and fog reduce both detection confidence and class separability. At night, overexposure from headlights can suppress texture and contour information, making visually similar vehicle classes harder to distinguish and increasing the risk of fragmented tracks or incorrect associations.

To handle this, separate tracker profiles were used for daytime and night-time operation, with adaptive switching during transitions based on image saturation signals. In addition, the detector was trained on mixed-light samples to improve robustness across illumination regimes. This strategy reduced sensitivity to non-stationary lighting and improved continuity in practical operation, although challenging night scenes with strong glare and occlusion still contribute to residual class- and route-level errors.


\subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}
The proposed tracking pipeline achieves performance comparable to, and in several aspects exceeding, earlier vision-based vehicle counting systems. Song et al.\ \cite{Song2019VisionBasedVehicle} reported average direction judgment accuracy of 92.3\% and overall counting accuracy of 93.2\% using YOLOv3 + ORB on highway videos with a simple detection-line approach. Our system, leveraging stronger YOLO26 detectors and state-of-the-art multi-object trackers, attains low aggregate vehicle-count errors (0.5\% for daytime, 1.5\% for night-time) and low errors across complex O–D routes in both daytime and night-time conditions at an intersection. Although direct metric comparison is limited by different evaluation targets (directional flow vs.\ full O–D matrix), the results demonstrate that the pipeline advances the state-of-the-art in robust, fine-grained traffic movement analysis under real-world variability.

\subsection{Runtime Feasibility}
To assess practical feasibility, we benchmarked the most lightweight end-to-end configuration (YOLO26n + ByteTrack) on a 15-minute daytime video segment. Using the exported ONNX model with ONNX Runtime, the complete model configuration achieved approximately 14 FPS on CPU. Since reliable tracking at the Haukeland station was previously achieved at 10 FPS, this suggests that CPU-only operation is a viable option for continuous deployment at comparable frame rates. While GPU acceleration remains preferable for higher-throughput processing, the CPU result strengthens the practical feasibility of long-term autonomous operation (RQ3) by reducing dependence on specialized hardware.


\section{Addressing Research Objectives}

This thesis was framed by three primary research questions (Section~\ref{sec:rq}). The findings directly address all:

\textbf{RQ1: How accurately can YOLO-based detection models extract fine-grained vehicle classification at an intersection?}

The first research objective focused on assessing the precision of YOLO-based models in extracting granular vehicle classification data. This was addressed by fine-tuning the YOLO26n and YOLO26m architectures on a custom-annotated dataset consisting of 1282 frames across eight distinct classes. The YOLO26m model demonstrated high performance, achieving a $mAP^{50}$ of 0.808 and an $mAP^{50-95}$ of 0.638. The model proved particularly effective at distinguishing the "new" heavy vehicles, with \textit{Semi-trailer/Combination Vehicle} reaching an $AP^{50}$ of 0.912 and \textit{Heavy Truck} reaching 0.817, while \textit{Light Truck} also performed strongly at 0.780. However, accuracy was lower for minority classes such as Bicycles, which reached an $AP^{50}$ of 0.495 primarily due to limited representation in the dataset. Analysis of confusion matrices indicates that errors are primarily caused by background interference and less by class confusion.

\textbf{RQ2: How do different model configurations (detector + tracker) perform in extracting
turning movement counts under varying lighting conditions?}

The second research objective assessed how reliably turning-movement counts can be extracted when combining the trained YOLO detector with different multi-object tracking configurations under both high- and low-visibility conditions. This was addressed by evaluating multiple detector--tracker pairings within the same zone-based Origin--Destination (O--D) framework on manually annotated video clips, where predicted O--D counts were compared against the ground truth.

Overall, the results show that the pipeline can achieve high counting accuracy in both lighting regimes, but that the optimal tracker settings are condition-dependent. Under daytime (high-visibility) conditions, the YOLO26m + Day BoT-SORT configuration produced the most consistent performance, yielding a net counting error of 0.5\% on the daytime validation sequence. Under nighttime (low-visibility) conditions, performance improved when using a more conservative, motion-dominant association strategy: the YOLO26m + Night BoT-SORT Conservative configuration was the best-performing option and achieved a net counting error of 1.5\% on the nighttime validation sequence. These findings indicate that accurate turning-movement estimation is feasible across varying illumination, provided that the tracker configuration is adapted to the visual characteristics of the scene.

\textbf{RQ3: To what extent can a vision-based traffic monitoring system operate autonomously over time without manual intervention while maintaining reliable performance?}

The final objective evaluated long-term autonomous viability and continuous operation under changing illumination. This was addressed by implementing an adaptive pipeline with Dynamic Tracker Switching, where the system monitors HSV saturation to automatically switch between day- and night-optimized tracking profiles without manual intervention. To maintain stable vehicle identities and consistent classification during the tracker switch, the pipeline further incorporated Split ID Recovery to merge fragmented trajectories. Together, these mechanisms support sustained autonomous operation without the need for manual intervention.

\section{Limitations of the Study}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}
The study is based on 1282 annotated frames collected primarily during February–March 2026. In early April, the dataset was expanded to include additional samples of vulnerable road users. However, despite this extension, their representation remains very limited. The test set contains only 2 bicycles and 6 motorcycles, which severely restricts the reliability of the model’s performance evaluation for these classes.

An additional limitation is that a strict held-out site evaluation was not performed. The train/validation/test partitioning was carried out at the frame level, meaning that both camera sites are represented in each split. While this provides an estimate of average in-domain performance, it may overestimate robustness to cross-site domain shifts. A more robust assessment would train on one station and test on the other to quantify generalization across locations.

A critical technical limitation stems from the automated privacy masking system implemented on SVV’s public live streams. While necessary for GDPR compliance, this system applies intermittent blurring and pixelation to vehicles. For detection, this is generally manageable since the model is trained on similarly degraded imagery. However, for object tracking, the masking introduces challenges when visual appearance changes abruptly, leading to ID switches rather than maintained continuity. This affects Origin–Destination tracking, where consistent vehicle identities are required from entry to exit.

These limitations are particularly severe at the Rv36 Kjørbekk camera station, which was therefore excluded from the object tracking analysis. In addition to the aggressive pixelation, often obscuring entire vehicles, the station operates at 5 FPS, resulting in large spatial gaps between frames. This combination leads to frequent ID switching and unreliable trajectory formation, especially for vehicles close to the camera. To further assess this limitation, tests conducted at 1 FPS resulted in a complete failure to assign any consistent IDs, confirming that reliable tracking is not feasible under such conditions. In contrast, the Haukeland station exhibits less severe masking and more stable visual continuity, making it suitable for tracking analysis.

The ground truth used to check the tracking pipeline and O-D matrices was made by manual counting. Especially for the one-hour rush hour video, this process is prone to human error. Issues like fatigue, high traffic volume, and cars crossing paths can lead to errors. Since the direction of this error is unknown, it is unclear if the manual count is too high or too low. Therefore, the actual performance might be slightly better or worse than what is reported.

\section{Practical Implications and Contributions}
A key practical contribution of this work is demonstrating that modern, lightweight YOLO-based detectors can provide a scalable alternative to labor-intensive traffic monitoring for many use cases, leveraging existing SVV camera infrastructure and standard GPU hardware rather than requiring dedicated sensors or manual counting. While camera-based systems do not replace all radar functions, they can substantially reduce the cost and effort of collecting high-frequency traffic flow and movement statistics.

In practical terms, the results show that the proposed pipeline performs reliably at the Haukeland station and can be transferred to other sites provided that basic video quality requirements are met, in particular sufficient frame rate (approximately $\geq$10 FPS) and acceptable visual continuity under privacy masking. Deployment at new intersections will likely require station-specific fine-tuning to account for local viewpoint and appearance differences, and careful definition of origin--destination zones aligned with the intersection geometry. Finally, the study highlights practical constraints associated with the public SVV stream configuration, where privacy masking and low frame rate can substantially reduce visual continuity. In an internal deployment setting, access to unmasked video would be expected to improve both detection and tracking reliability, particularly at challenging stations such as Rv36 Kjørbekk.


\section{Future Research}
One key direction for future work is systematically investigating the minimum frame rate threshold for reliable object tracking. This study identified complete failure at 1 FPS and severe degradation at 5 FPS, but the exact threshold and its relationship to object velocity remains unclear. Future work should establish quantitative frame rate–performance curves across 1–30 FPS, investigate how object velocity and camera proximity affect frame rate requirements, and explore frame interpolation techniques to enable tracking on lower-FPS systems. This is particularly relevant in practice, as many existing CCTV-based traffic monitoring systems operate under limited frame rate conditions, and improving performance under such constraints is essential for maximizing the utility of existing infrastructure.

Another important area is to further improve the object detection model itself. This thesis shows that tracking and O–D counting accuracy are strongly dependent on detection quality: missed or incorrect detections propagate directly into fragmented tracks and counting errors, and these errors cannot be fully corrected by changing the tracker alone. Future work should therefore focus on improving detection robustness in challenging situations such as occlusions, dense traffic, and low-light conditions, as this is likely to yield large gains in end-to-end performance.

Finally, future research should focus on improving robustness to occlusions and imperfect tracking. Although this thesis introduces practical logic to mitigate ID switches and recover split trajectories (e.g., through zone-based O–D counting and post-processing of fragmented tracks), these safeguards do not fully eliminate errors in dense traffic and prolonged occlusion events. Future work should therefore investigate more reliable strategies for maintaining consistent identities through occlusions, and develop more systematic post-processing methods for merging fragmented tracks, with the aim of further reducing undercounting and route-level errors in real-world intersection monitoring.
