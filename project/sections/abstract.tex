\thispagestyle{frontmatter}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Traffic monitoring at intersections provides inputs to Operations Research and Industrial Economics tasks in planning, operations, and safety management. However, current approaches (e.g., manual counting and inductive loops) are limited in scalability, cost, and the level of detail they can provide. In line with the National Transport Plan’s emphasis on digitalization, this thesis investigates whether roadside camera video from the Norwegian Public Roads Administration’s (SVV) infrastructure can be converted into decision-relevant traffic measures without installing new sensors.

The objective is to evaluate how accurately a deep learning-based detection and tracking pipeline can (i) classify vehicles into detailed categories, (ii) estimate turning movements under varying lighting conditions, and (iii) operate autonomously during day--night transitions.

Methodologically, we fine-tune YOLO26n and YOLO26m on a custom dataset of 1282 manually annotated frames from two Norwegian intersections (8 classes). The detector is integrated into a tracking-by-detection pipeline (ByteTrack and BoT-SORT) and combined with a zone-based origin--destination framework to estimate turning movements from trajectories. To support continuous operation, an adaptive dual-tracker mechanism switches between day- and night-optimized configurations based on real-time lighting analysis. End-to-end performance is evaluated against manual ground truth on 1-hour daytime and nighttime video segments from a real-world intersection.

YOLO26m achieves $mAP^{50}=0.808$ on the independent test set (YOLO26n: 0.693). For the end-to-end pipeline, the best configurations reach vehicle counting accuracies of 99.5\% (day) and 98.5\% (night) on the 1-hour segments.

The main contribution is evidence that high-quality detection is the primary driver of end-to-end traffic-parameter accuracy, while tracker choice and tuning mainly influence error allocation across routes and minority classes. For practice, the results indicate that video-based monitoring can complement existing infrastructure by providing scalable estimates of vehicle composition and turning movements. In OR terms, this supports using video as an operational measurement layer for turning-movement estimation and related KPIs. This requires that video quality and site calibration requirements are met. Broader deployment will require further validation, including stronger evidence on cross-site generalization and performance for vulnerable road users.
