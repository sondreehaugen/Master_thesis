\section{Traffic Data Collection Methods and Limitations}
\label{sec:traffic_data_collection_methods}
This section summarizes how traffic data are collected in Norway and the main limitations of these methods. It then reviews international practice to show how other countries approach automated traffic registrations and vehicle classification, including the role of video- and AI-based systems.

\subsection{Current Practice in Norway}
Traffic data can be collected either manually or by automated technologies. The following subsections outline the most common methods used in Norway and summarize their main strengths and limitations, with a focus on the type and quality of data they provide.

\subsubsection{Manual Traffic Registrations}
Manual registrations involve personnel recording data on forms or digital devices \citep{SVV_V714}. These registrations are well suited to detailed, short-term observations, typically lasting 1-4 hours during peak periods, and can capture turning movements with high accuracy. However, the method is labour-intensive and prone to human error, particularly in complex environments with high volumes or multiple turning movements. Capturing full temporal variation across time of day, weekdays, or seasons requires substantial manual labor and operational resources. This means that manual counts typically provide short-term snapshots rather than continuous behavioral datasets. \citep{SVV_V714}.

\subsubsection{Automated Traffic Registrations}

\textbf{Inductive loop detectors} consist of insulated wire loops installed in saw-cut slots in the pavement. When a vehicle passes over, its metal mass disturbs the electromagnetic field, registering a detection event \citep{SVV_V714}. They generally provide high accuracy for traffic volume under normal conditions, but vehicles stopping or moving slowly over the loop during congestion may cause underestimations or misclassifications. The embedded installation makes loops vulnerable to winter maintenance operations such as road milling and snow removal, resulting in costly repairs and temporary data loss. They also provide limited vehicle classification detail.

\textbf{Radar sensors} employ the Doppler effect to detect vehicle speed and direction by transmitting pulses and analyzing the frequency shift in reflected signals \citep{SVV_V714}. In high-density traffic, overlapping targets and signal interference can reduce accuracy. Classification capabilities are limited in practice and depend heavily on calibration. Sensors must be carefully positioned to avoid queue areas near intersections, and while radar can technically detect pedestrians and cyclists, such applications are rarely implemented operationally.

\textbf{Piezoelectric cables} are pressure-sensitive wires laid transversely across lanes. They generate a voltage proportional to the applied force, enabling axle counts, speed, weight, and spacing measurements \citep{SVV_V714}. These cables are primarily suited to short-term deployments of only a few days, as they are exposed to mechanical wear and degrade quickly. Winter conditions and snow plowing increase the risk of damage, and installation typically requires temporary road closure.

\textbf{Pneumatic road tubes} are rubber hoses mounted temporarily across the road surface, converting axle-generated air pulses into electrical signals \citep{SVV_V714}. A single tube registers axle counts rather than individual vehicles, which can overestimate volumes for heavy vehicles. Two tubes allow speed and direction to be derived, but the method remains limited to short-term use due to rapid wear and vulnerability to winter conditions, with installation again requiring temporary road closure.

\subsubsection{LiDAR}
LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active sensing technology that emits laser pulses and records reflected signals to generate three-dimensional point clouds representing the surrounding environment. LiDAR operates continuously and is largely independent of ambient lighting conditions \citep{Lv2019_LiDAR}. Although not part of the Norwegian Public Roads Administration's (SVV) standard counting infrastructure, it has been tested in pilot projects. This includes the Borealis ITS test corridor on E8 in Skibotndalen and short-term intersection trials combining mobile cameras and LiDAR for movement analysis \citep{SVV_ITS_teststrekninger, SVV_2025_kryss_lidar_test}. Research further demonstrates that static roadside LiDAR combined with deep learning can enable detailed scene understanding and road user classification at intersections \citep{LiDAR_IEEE}. However, LiDAR hardware remains costly, and point cloud processing pipelines are computationally demanding, limiting widespread operational deployment.


\subsubsection{Camera-Based Traffic Registration}
Camera-based traffic registration uses video feeds from roadside cameras to automatically 
detect, classify, and register road users. An AI model processes the video stream in real 
time, assigns detected vehicles to predefined classes and produces continuous automated record of traffic volume and composition. Unlike methods requiring physical sensors, camera-based systems can use existing installations already in place for 
surveillance or safety purposes. In addition, it can operate continuously without disturbing road infrastructure.

Camera-based image analysis is not entirely new to SVV. They already operate Automatic Number Plate Recognition (ANPR) systems at roadside control points \citep{SVV_ANPR}. They have also piloted AI-based 
incident detection in tunnels, where existing camera infrastructure was used to 
automatically detect stopped vehicles and pedestrians \citep{SVV_AI_tunnel}. However, 
neither application produces systematic traffic registration data such as volume, class 
distribution, or movement patterns. The approach explored in this thesis extends 
camera-based analysis into that domain.

Performance can degrade under poor lighting, challenging weather and occlusion in dense traffic. Accuracy must therefore be validated empirically before camera-based registration can serve as an alternative to established methods.

\subsubsection{Overall Limitations}
Traditional traffic data collection methods are primarily designed to measure aggregated vehicle parameters at fixed points. While they provide reliable volume and speed estimates, they offer limited insight into spatial traffic dynamics, turning trajectories, and behavioral patterns at intersections. The detection of pedestrians and cyclists is either limited or not operationally implemented, and interaction-based safety indicators cannot be derived from conventional sensor data. This structural gap motivates the investigation of AI-based computer vision methods. These methods may enable automated and scalable extraction of detailed traffic metrics from existing camera infrastructure.


\subsection{International Practice}
\label{sec:international_practice}
Internationally, Germany provides a well-documented example of formal approval regimes for automated traffic registrations and vehicle classification. Systems such as the TOPO platform are certified by the Federal Highway Research Institute (BASt) and are based on non-intrusive sensor technologies, including radar and LiDAR, rather than video-based deep learning. These systems classify vehicles according to classes defined in German technical specifications and are approved for use in official traffic registrations \citep{RTBGmbHCoKG2019}.

In the United States, traffic data collection relies on a mix of technologies with limited use of video-based systems. Historically, traditional video image detection (VID) systems have been capable of collecting and analyzing standard traffic data, but with important limitations: their classification ability is largely restricted to daylight operation, and they typically only support three to five length-based vehicle classes. This makes them unsuitable for axle-based schemes such as those required by the FHWA \citep{FHWA2014VID}. Recent guidance from the Washington State Department of Transportation indicates that inductive loops and pneumatic tubes remain the dominant technologies for traffic registrations and vehicle classification, with video not established as a standard method \citep{WSDOT2024}. At the same time, some local authorities are applying AI to existing CCTV infrastructure for traffic and pedestrian monitoring, demonstrating a potential pathway for cost-effective use of computer vision without new sensor deployments \citep{ITSKRSComputerVisionCCTV}.

In the UK, local authorities have documented the operational use of video-based traffic sensors combined with AI. Cambridgeshire County Council reports the deployment of Vivacity sensors to collect classified traffic flow data for transport planning and decision-making, using AI applied to video to count and classify vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists \citep{VivacityValidation2022}. 

These examples indicate that while video-based and AI-enabled systems are increasingly used for traffic monitoring and data collection, this is typically in combination with other technologies or within pilot and local operational contexts. No evidence has been identified of national road authorities relying solely on video-based data for vehicle classification as a normative basis for traffic data collection.

\section{Deep Learning and Computer Vision}
\label{sec:computer_vision}
This section introduces the deep learning and computer vision concepts supporting the detection and tracking pipeline developed in this thesis, covering convolutional neural networks, object detection architectures, YOLO-based models, and multi-object tracking.

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations of Machine Learning and Computer Vision}
\label{sec:ml_cv}
Machine learning (ML) is a subfield of artificial intelligence in which algorithms infer decision logic directly from data, rather than relying on hand-crafted rules \citep{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. Within this field, computer vision (CV) concerns the automatic interpretation of visual information from images and video. It enables the extraction of semantic content, including object identity, spatial position and motion, from raw pixel data \citep{Szeliski2010}.

Recent advances in deep learning have substantially improved the robustness of computer vision systems to real-world variability in illumination, viewpoint, and occlusion by learning hierarchical feature representations from large-scale image datasets \citep{LeCun2015}. These properties are particularly relevant for traffic monitoring in complex outdoor environments, where conditions change continuously and manual data collection is both costly and inconsistent.

Vision-based analysis tasks are commonly categorized into three progressively more complex problem types, each building on the previous, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:Classification}. \textbf{Image classification} assigns a single semantic label to an entire image but provides no spatial information about object location \citep{Krizhevsky2012}. \textbf{Object detection} extends this by simultaneously identifying object categories and localizing each instance through bounding boxes. This capability is essential for characterizing the spatial distribution of vehicles, cyclists, and pedestrians at intersections \citep{Ma2024}. \textbf{Object tracking} further associates detections across consecutive video frames, enabling the reconstruction of object trajectories over time \citep{Bewley2016}. For intersection monitoring, this progression is critical. Detection alone cannot capture movement patterns, and it is only through tracking that frame-level observations are transformed into actionable traffic data such as turning movement counts and vehicle class distributions.

\subsection{Deep Learning and Convolutional Neural Networks}
\label{sec:dl_cnn}

Deep learning is a class of machine learning methods based on artificial neural networks with multiple hidden layers, enabling the learning of complex, hierarchical representations from large-scale data \citep{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}. In contrast to shallow models, deep learning architectures are particularly effective for high-dimensional inputs such as images and video, where relevant features must be extracted directly from raw pixel data.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) represent the foundational deep learning architecture for computer vision. CNNs exploit the spatial structure of image data through convolutional layers, which apply learnable filters to local patches of the input, typically of size 3$\times$3 or 5$\times$5 pixels. Because the local statistics of images are invariant to location, all units in a feature map share the same filters, enabling the network to detect the same patterns regardless of their position in the image \citep{LeCun2015}.

To model complex decision boundaries, convolutional layers are combined with non-linear activation functions, most commonly the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) \citep{Nair2010}. Without multiple stages of non-linearity, the network would collapse into a linear model and be unable to learn the hierarchical representations required for visual discrimination \citep{LeCun2015}. Pooling layers are inserted between convolutional blocks to progressively downsample the spatial dimensions of feature maps, reducing computational cost in later layers \citep{OSheaNash2015}.

Finally, the high-level features produced by the convolutional and pooling layers are passed to fully connected layers, which aggregate these representations and produce the final classification output \citep{OSheaNash2015}. Together, these components form a hierarchical pipeline in which early layers capture low-level features such as edges and textures, while deeper layers encode increasingly abstract semantic concepts relevant to the classification task, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:CNN}.

\subsection{Object Detection Architectures}
\label{sec:object_detection}

Object detection is a core computer vision task that integrates image classification, identifying the category of an object, with spatial localization, determining its position within the image through bounding box regression \citep{Zhao2019}. For traffic monitoring applications, this dual capability is essential: detected road users must be assigned precise spatial positions to enable downstream trajectory estimation and traffic flow analysis.

Deep learning object detection models are commonly categorized into two paradigms based on their localization strategy: two-stage and one-stage detectors.

\subsubsection{Two-Stage Object Detection}

Two-stage detectors decompose the detection task into sequential steps: region proposal generation followed by per-region classification and bounding box refinement \citep{Girshick2014, Ren2015}. Candidate object regions are first generated by a Region Proposal Network (RPN), and subsequently refined in a second stage. This design yields high localization accuracy and strong performance on small or partially occluded objects, at the cost of increased computational complexity and reduced inference speed \citep{Zhao2019}. Extensions such as Mask R-CNN further augment this paradigm with instance-level segmentation capabilities, broadening the range of spatial analysis tasks that can be performed \citep{He2017}. However, the multi-stage pipeline makes two-stage detectors generally unsuitable for continuous video processing where real-time throughput is required.

\subsubsection{One-Stage Object Detection}

One-stage detectors formulate detection as a single unified regression and classification problem, performing bounding box prediction and object categorization simultaneously across the image in a single forward pass \citep{Redmon2016, Liu2016}. The elimination of the region proposal stage results in substantially lower inference latency and higher processing throughput, making one-stage architectures well suited for real-time video analysis. Early one-stage models exhibited reduced localization accuracy relative to two-stage approaches, particularly for small or densely clustered objects \citep{Zhao2019}. However, successive architectural advances, including feature pyramid networks, anchor-free regression heads, and attention mechanisms, have substantially narrowed this performance gap \citep{Bochkovskiy2020}, making modern one-stage detectors competitive with two-stage models across standard detection benchmarks.

\subsection{YOLO-Based Object Detection}
\label{sec:yolo_models}

The You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of models represents the most widely adopted class of one-stage object detectors. A defining characteristic of the framework is its emphasis on global image reasoning: rather than processing candidate regions independently, YOLO considers the full image context during detection, contributing to reduced false positives in visually complex scenes \citep{Redmon2016}. This design enables end-to-end training and real-time inference, making YOLO-based models particularly well suited for continuous video processing applications.

Since its introduction in 2016, the YOLO family has undergone continuous architectural development, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:yolo_timeline}, 
with the most recent model being YOLO26, released in 2026 \citep{Sapkota2025YOLOReview}. Key advances include the introduction of anchor boxes to improve localization stability (YOLOv2), multi-scale feature prediction to enable detection across a range of object sizes (YOLOv3), and improved feature aggregation and training strategies to strengthen the speed-accuracy trade-off (YOLOv4) \citep{Sapkota2025YOLOReview}. Later generations introduced anchor-free detection heads and decoupled classification and regression branches (YOLOv8), as well as end-to-end optimization with reduced post-processing overhead in more recent variants \citep{Sapkota2025YOLOReview}. Collectively, these developments reflect a consistent progression toward improved multi-scale detection, greater generalization, and more flexible deployment across a range of hardware configurations.

multi-scale detection capability of modern YOLO variants enables simultaneous 
detection of objects across a range of sizes and distances within a single 
camera frame. This is essential when monitoring intersections where vehicles 
of varying dimensions, cyclists, and pedestrians must all be reliably 
identified.

\subsection{YOLO26 Key Advancements}
\label{sec:yolo26-advancements}

YOLO26 models are pre-trained on the COCO dataset (Common Objects in Context), which was introduced by \cite{COCO_dataset} as a large-scale object detection benchmark. The dataset was later refined into the COCO 2017 version, which includes 80 object categories across 330,000 images with 200,000 annotated for object detection, segmentation, and captioning tasks. The dataset is split into three subsets: Train2017 (118K images), Val2017 (5K images), and Test2017 (20K images)\citep{ultralytics2024coco}. YOLO models are pre-trained on the COCO training set to leverage learned representations from diverse object categories before fine-tuning on task-specific detection tasks.

YOLO26 architectural details and initial benchmarks were first detailed in a September 2025 preprint \citep{sapkota2026yolo26keyarchitecturalenhancements}, with the full public release of models occurring in January 2026. Regarding its distribution, the model is provided under a dual-licensing scheme. It is available under the open-source AGPL-3.0 license for researchers and open-source projects, while an Enterprise License is required for proprietary commercial applications \citep{Ultralytics2024}.

A primary advancement in YOLO26 is its fully end-to-end inference pipeline that eliminates Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) entirely. By removing this traditional post-processing step for duplicate bounding boxes, the model achieves substantially lower latency. When combined with the removal of Distribution Focal Loss (DFL), which simplifies bounding-box regression, YOLO26 delivers up to 43\% faster CPU inference compared with previous YOLO versions \citep{sapkota2026yolo26keyarchitecturalenhancements}. These changes make the architecture particularly well-suited for real-time applications on resource-constrained hardware.

Furthermore, YOLO26 introduces Progressive Loss Balancing (ProgLoss) and Small-Target-Aware Label Assignment (STAL) to improve training dynamics and small-object performance. ProgLoss adaptively reweights the individual loss terms throughout training, reducing the dominance of easy examples and promoting more balanced learning. STAL, in turn, gives higher priority to small or heavily occluded targets during label assignment. In the context of traffic surveillance, this noticeably enhances the detection of distant or partially obscured vehicles. Finally, the novel MuSGD optimizer accelerates convergence while improving stability. This simplifies fine-tuning on custom vehicle datasets and reduces sensitivity to hyperparameter choices \citep{sapkota2026yolo26keyarchitecturalenhancements}.

\subsection{YOLO26 Architecture Overview}
\label{sec:yolo26_architecture}
YOLO26 follows the classic single-stage, end-to-end object detection paradigm introduced in earlier Ultralytics YOLO versions. The network is composed of three main modules: backbone, neck, and head, and it processes an input image of size $640 \times 640 \times 3$. The architecture is defined in the official Ultralytics \texttt{yolo26.yaml} configuration file and is fully described in the comprehensive analysis by Hidayatullah and Tubagus (2026). \autoref{fig:YOLO26_architecture} illustrates the complete CNN-based architecture of YOLO26, including the flow of feature maps and the location of the key components.

The model supports five different scales (nano, small, medium, large, and extra-large) that are controlled by three scaling parameters: \texttt{depth\_multiple}, \texttt{width\_multiple}, and \texttt{max\_channels}.

\subsubsection*{Building blocks}
Before describing the backbone, neck, and head, the main building blocks used in the YOLO26 diagram are briefly defined. Conv block denotes a convolutional layer combined with normalization and an activation function. YOLO26 further relies on repeated feature-extraction blocks (C3k2), which are built from convolutional bottleneck-style submodules. Here, a bottleneck refers to a compact sub-block that reduces and then expands the channel dimension, enabling efficient feature learning with lower computational cost \citep{he2015deepresiduallearningimage}. SPPF (Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast) aggregates features at multiple pooling scales to increase the effective receptive field. C2PSA adds an attention mechanism (self-attention) to strengthen global context modeling \citep{hidayatullah2026yolo26}.

\subsubsection*{Backbone}
The backbone of YOLO26 is responsible for hierarchical feature extraction from the input image (640 $\times$ 640 $\times$ 3). It consists of an initial pair of convolutional layers (kernel size 3, stride 2) that progressively reduce spatial resolution, followed by a sequence of C3k2 blocks (parameterized by \texttt{depth\_multiple}, \texttt{width\_multiple}, and \texttt{max\_channels}) interleaved with additional convolutional blocks \citep{hidayatullah2026yolo26}. The final stages include an enhanced SPPF module with a shortcut connection and a C2PSA block. The last three backbone stages (P3, P4, P5) forward multi-scale feature maps to the neck.

In general, the backbone's main objective is to progressively transform the raw input image into rich, multi-scale feature representations, capturing low-level details (edges, textures) in shallow layers and high-level semantic information (object context) in deeper layers.

\subsubsection*{Neck}
The neck of YOLO26 aggregates and fuses the multi-scale feature maps produced by the backbone. It begins with the SPPF module, followed by a C2PSA attention block, then alternates between upsampling (nearest-neighbor), concatenation, and additional C3k2 blocks \citep{hidayatullah2026yolo26}. Three refined feature maps (at 80 $\times$ 80, 40 $\times$ 40, and 20 $\times$ 20 resolution) are passed forward to the head.

The neck's main objective is to combine semantic information from deeper layers with fine-grained spatial details from shallower layers. This multi-scale feature combination enables the model to detect objects effectively at different sizes.

\subsubsection*{Head}
The head of YOLO26 consists of three parallel detection branches connected to the neck outputs (P3 for small objects, P4 for medium objects, and P5 for large objects). Each branch comprises a C3k2 block followed by a final convolutional layer before the Detect module, which outputs bounding boxes, class probabilities, and objectness scores in an end-to-end, NMS-free manner \citep{hidayatullah2026yolo26}.

The head's main objective is to translate the fused multi-scale features into the final set of predictions, which include bounding-box coordinates, confidence scores, and class labels.

\subsection{Multi-Object Tracking and Trajectory Extraction}
\label{sec:multi_object_tracking}

Measuring traffic parameters such as turning movements goes beyond object detection; it requires Multi-Object Tracking (MOT). MOT 
assigns a persistent and unique identifier to each detected road user across 
consecutive video frames, enabling trajectory estimation and movement 
analysis \citep{Hassan2024}. In practice, most MOT systems follow a 
tracking-by-detection paradigm, where per-frame detections are linked over 
time using a combination of motion modeling and data association 
\citep{yang2022videoobjecttrackingbased}. The two core components underlying 
this paradigm are Kalman filtering for motion prediction and the Hungarian 
algorithm for optimal detection-to-track assignment. Modern trackers such as 
ByteTrack and BoT-SORT build on this foundation with more robust association 
strategies.

\subsubsection*{Kalman Filtering}

The Kalman filter is a recursive estimator that predicts and refines the 
state of a dynamic system over time under noisy observations and process 
uncertainty \citep{Kalman1960}. In the MOT context, each active tracklet (a short track for one object across frames)
maintains a state vector encoding its bounding box position, size, and 
velocity. The filter operates in two alternating steps per frame. First, 
in the \emph{prediction} step, the filter extrapolates the tracklet's 
current state forward using a constant-velocity motion model, producing an 
estimated position and an associated uncertainty. Second, in the 
\emph{update} step, this prediction is refined using the matched detection 
as a measurement. The Kalman gain determines how much weight is placed on
the prediction relative to the observation \citep{WelchBishop2006}. 
Together, these steps allow the tracker to anticipate object positions 
between frames, handle brief missed detections, and reduce noise in 
bounding box estimates.

\subsubsection*{Hungarian Matching}

Given a set of predicted tracklet positions and a set of new detections, 
the data association problem reduces to finding the optimal one-to-one 
assignment between the two sets. This is solved by the Hungarian algorithm 
\citep{Kuhn1955, Munkres1957}, which finds the minimum-cost assignment 
efficiently. The cost matrix is typically constructed from Intersection 
over Union (IoU) between predicted and detected bounding boxes, so that 
spatially consistent pairs receive low cost. This combination of Kalman 
filtering and Hungarian matching was first applied to online tracking by 
\citet{Bewley2016} in the SORT framework. It forms the foundation that most subsequent trackers build upon.

\subsubsection*{ByteTrack}

ByteTrack is an online tracking-by-detection method that improves robustness 
to missed detections by explicitly incorporating \emph{low-confidence} 
detections into the association process \citep{Zhang2021ByteTrack}. Instead 
of discarding detections below a fixed confidence threshold, ByteTrack 
performs a two-stage matching procedure. In the first stage, high-confidence 
detections are associated with existing tracklets using Kalman-predicted 
positions and IoU-based Hungarian assignment. In the second stage, any 
remaining unmatched tracklets are matched against lower-confidence detections, 
allowing partially occluded or blurred objects to be recovered when their 
detector score temporarily drops. By treating low-score boxes as potentially 
informative rather than noise, ByteTrack reduces trajectory fragmentation 
and improves identity continuity under partial occlusion or low visibility 
conditions.

\subsubsection*{BoT-SORT}

BoT-SORT extends the SORT framework by strengthening both motion modeling 
and data association \citep{Aharon2022BoTSORT}. It retains the standard 
online tracking loop (Kalman prediction, Hungarian assignment, track 
management) but introduces an enhanced Kalman filter state representation 
for more accurate bounding box prediction. In its full configuration 
(BoT-SORT-ReID), the Hungarian cost matrix is augmented with 
appearance-based re-identification (ReID) embeddings, which can improve 
identity preservation when geometric cues are ambiguous. However, the 
benefit of ReID depends on stable visual appearance and may diminish under 
strong illumination changes, motion blur, or privacy-masking artifacts 
common in real-world traffic video.

\section{Performance Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}
To quantitatively assess the effectiveness of the YOLO architecture on the traffic dataset, several standard computer vision metrics are employed. These metrics evaluate the model's ability to both correctly localize and accurately classify vehicles \citep{vijayakumar2024yolo}.

\subsection*{Intersection over Union (IoU)}
The fundamental building block for evaluating detection accuracy is Intersection over Union (IoU). This metric measures the overlap between the predicted bounding box (\(B_p\)) and the ground truth box (\(B_{gt}\)) provided during the manual annotation. It is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
IoU = \frac{\text{Area}(B_p \cap B_{gt})}{\text{Area}(B_p \cup B_{gt})}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Precision and Recall}
Precision quantifies the ratio of correct detections relative to the total number of times the model claimed to have found an object. A high precision score indicates that the model rarely misidentifies background noise or incorrect vehicle types as a target class.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:precision}
\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\end{equation}

Recall, often referred to as the True Positive Rate or sensitivity, determines how many of the actual vehicles present in the ground truth data were successfully identified by the model. A high recall is vital to ensure that as few vehicles as possible pass through the frame undetected.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recall}
\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Average Precision (AP) and Mean Average Precision (mAP)}
Average Precision (AP) is defined as the area under the Precision-Recall Curve. It is a single numerical metric that summarizes the trade-off between precision and recall across all possible confidence thresholds for a specific object class. A high AP indicates that the model maintains both high precision and recall for a specific class. The AP is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
AP = \int_{0}^{1} p(r) \, dr
\end{equation}

Where \(p(r)\) is the precision as a function of recall.

While the AP measures the performance for a single class, Mean Average Precision (mAP) is the standard metric used to evaluate the overall performance of a multi-class object detection model like YOLO. If N represents the total number of classes, the mAP is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mAP_calc}
mAP = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} AP_i
\end{equation}

The literature commonly reports two versions of mAP to provide a complete picture of model performance. The mAP$^{50}$ represents the mAP calculated at a fixed IoU threshold of 0.5. This means that a detection is labeled as a True Positive if the overlap between the predicted box and the GT box is at least 50\%. Instead of using a single threshold, mAP$^{50-95}$ uses the average of mAP values calculated at ten different IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95, in 0.05 increments. This metric rewards models for superior localization accuracy.

\subsection*{MOT Evaluation Metrics}
\label{evaluation_metrics}

Standard MOT metrics such as MOTA, MOTP, and IDF1 penalize detection misses, false positives, localization errors, and identity switches \citep{JIMENEZBRAVO202243}. However, they require dense frame-by-frame identity annotations, making them impractical when analyzing thousands of frames. Instead, we evaluate pipelines using task-specific metrics derived from manually annotated ground-truth Origin-Destination (O-D) matrices, where $\hat{S}_r$ and $S_r$ are the predicted and ground-truth total counts for route $r$, $\hat{T}_{r,c}$ and $T_{r,c}$ are the predicted and ground-truth counts for route $r$ and vehicle class $c$, and $\mathcal{R}^+$ denotes routes with $S_r > 0$.

The net signed difference between total predicted and ground-truth counts across all routes indicates systematic over- or under-counting:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{total}} = \sum_r \hat{S}_r - \sum_r S_r \label{eq:total_error}
\end{equation}

Normalizing the absolute total error by the ground-truth count allows performance to be compared across videos with different object counts:
\begin{equation}
\%\mathrm{Err}_{\text{abs}} = \frac{|E_{\text{total}}|}{\sum_r S_r} \cdot 100 \label{eq:pct_error}
\end{equation}

The mean absolute deviation across all route--class cells measures class-level counting accuracy:
\begin{equation}
\%\mathrm{Err}_{\text{class}} = \frac{\sum_{r,c} |\hat{T}_{r,c} - T_{r,c}|}{\sum_{r,c} T_{r,c}} \cdot 100 \label{eq:class_rel_error}
\end{equation}

The average per-route relative error across active routes captures directional counting accuracy independently of route volume:
\begin{equation}
\%\mathrm{Err}_{\text{route}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}^+|} \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}^+} \frac{|\hat{S}_r - S_r|}{S_r} \cdot 100 \label{eq:route_rel_error}
\end{equation}

Together, these metrics quantify overall counting reliability, class-specific performance, and per-route directional accuracy, directly reflecting how well the pipeline reproduces aggregated traffic movements.

\section{Literature Review and Knowledge Gap}
\label{sec:literature_review_research_gap}

This section reviews prior studies on deep learning–based vehicle detection for traffic monitoring, focusing on detection performance, computational efficiency, and real-world applicability. Identified limitations in existing work form the basis for the knowledge gaps addressed in this thesis.

\subsection{Prior Work on Vehicle Detection and Tracking}
\label{sec:previous_work}
Several studies have investigated the performance of deep learning models for vehicle detection and classification using established benchmark datasets. For example, \cite{Ma2024} evaluated multiple one-stage and two-stage detection models on the PASCAL VOC 2012 dataset, a commonly used dataset in Computer Vision \citep{Everingham10,HuggingFacePascalVOC}. Although the dataset consists of still images rather than video sequences, the authors report that some one-stage models achieve processing speeds of up to 45 frames per second, but with lower detection accuracy compared to slower two-stage models. The study primarily formulates vehicle detection as a generic object detection task, and the number of vehicle classes used for classification is not explicitly specified.


\cite{smartcities6050134} performed a large-scale comparison of Single Shot Detector (SSD), Faster R-CNN, and multiple YOLO variants using video data from 9 different datasets, recorded under diverse conditions, including nighttime, adverse weather, occlusions, and varying camera viewpoints. Their results show that YOLO-based models consistently outperform SSD and Faster R-CNN in terms of detection accuracy, localization precision, and computational efficiency. In particular, YOLO variants achieved detection accuracies above 95\% and overall classification accuracies exceeding 90\% for cars, trucks, and buses, while maintaining near real-time inference speeds. Although Faster R-CNN demonstrated competitive performance in some localization tasks, its substantially higher computational cost limited its suitability for continuous video-based traffic surveillance. The study therefore concludes that YOLO-based architectures provide the most favorable trade-off between accuracy and efficiency for real-time traffic monitoring applications.

\cite{Song2019VisionBasedVehicle} trained and evaluated a vision-based vehicle detection and counting system using YOLOv3 for object detection in combination with Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF (ORB), a feature detection and description algorithm, for multi-object tracking (MOT). The ORB algorithm extracts features within each detected bounding box and matches them across consecutive frames to maintain object correspondence over time. The dataset consisted of images captured from a variety of viewpoints and included three vehicle classes: cars, buses, and trucks. The authors report a mean average precision (mAP) of 87.88\%, with very small class deviations from this value. Using ORB-based tracking, the system achieved a vehicle counting accuracy of 93.2\% and a direction recognition accuracy of 92.3\%, while maintaining processing speeds close to real time. Although these results demonstrate strong performance for traffic monitoring, the study is limited to highway environments and a small number of vehicle classes.

Fewer studies go beyond coarse vehicle categories and investigate fine-grained vehicle classification in traffic-camera footage. For example, \cite{Neupane2022RealTimeVehicle} developed a real-time system combining YOLO-based detection with a lightweight tracking-by-detection algorithm to produce lane-level traffic statistics. To reduce domain shift, the authors trained an initial model on a larger multi-camera dataset (TR1) and then fine-tuned it on a smaller deployment-specific dataset (TR2), reporting validation performance on TR2 of $mAP^{50}\approx0.67$--$0.71$ across several YOLO variants. Beyond the COCO vehicle labels, the system expands the label space to seven traffic-relevant classes (car, bus, taxi, bike, pickup, truck, and trailer) and achieved approximately 95\% overall accuracy when integrated into the full tracking pipeline.

\cite{YOLO-PEL} propose YOLOv8-PEL, a lightweight vehicle detection model built upon YOLOv8n, the smallest variant in the YOLOv8 family. The authors introduce architectural modifications aimed at improving multi-scale feature extraction and feature fusion, as well as a revised bounding box regression loss function to enhance localization stability. These changes reduce the number of model parameters and computational cost by approximately 25–30\% compared to the baseline YOLOv8n, while maintaining nearly identical mean average precision (mAP). The results demonstrate that lightweight architectural modifications can significantly improve real-time suitability without substantial loss in detection accuracy.

\subsection{Knowledge Gaps and Steps for Further Research}
\label{sec:research_gap}

\cite{Maity2022LastDecadeVehicleDetection} present a comprehensive survey of machine learning and deep learning-based approaches for automatic vehicle detection (AVD) and classification published over the last decade. Their review identifies several knowledge gaps. In particular, they note that many studies consider only a limited number of vehicle classes, motivating broader coverage to better reflect real-world traffic diversity, and that robustness is often not explicitly evaluated under operationally common conditions such as occlusion and dense traffic.

Related work suggests that these limitations persist for modern YOLO-based detectors. \cite{DeepLearningTrafficScene2025} emphasize that improvements are still needed for small-object and multi-scale detection, as well as robustness under adverse environmental conditions such as challenging lighting and weather. In addition, \cite{YOLO-PEL} argue that future research should prioritize practical validation in real-world deployment settings in order to assess operational reliability and applicability.

In the context of Multi-Object Detection (MOT), \cite{Hassan2024} conducts a systematic literature review of MOT approaches and notes that future work should explore deep learning-based tracking with an emphasis on more end-to-end MOT solutions to improve robustness and accuracy in complex real-world scenarios. Additionally, the authors emphasize that future research should focus on dealing with occlusions; a frequent problem that leads to ID-switches.

To address these identified gaps, this thesis applies a deep learning-based, end-to-end vision pipeline to video data from fixed roadside cameras in real intersection environments. The approach covers the full workflow from video input to automatically extracted traffic measures, and the study expands beyond standard benchmark evaluations by focusing on a broader range of traffic participants. Furthermore, detection performance is evaluated under realistic operational conditions, including varying lighting, traffic density, and partial occlusion, and the pipeline incorporates practical strategies to mitigate the impact of occlusions during continuous operation.

The extent to which these knowledge gaps are addressed is evaluated by quantitatively comparing the automatically extracted traffic parameters (traffic volumes, vehicle composition, and turning movements) against the performance levels and limitations reported in prior studies, using accuracy metrics and error analysis.
