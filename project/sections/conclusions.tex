\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
This thesis evaluates whether deep learning-based detection and tracking can extract reliable traffic parameters from existing roadside video at a real-world Norwegian intersection. The results provide clear answers to the three research questions. For \textbf{RQ1}, fine-tuned YOLO26 models can support detailed vehicle classification in this setting. The best-performing model (YOLO26m) achieves strong overall detection accuracy on the independent test set and shows particularly robust performance for heavy-vehicle subclasses. For \textbf{RQ2}, turning-movement counts can be estimated with low aggregate error in both daytime and nighttime conditions. This holds when the tracker configuration is adapted to the visual regime. For \textbf{RQ3}, autonomous operation through illumination transitions is feasible. The adaptive dual-tracker mechanism switches between day- and night-optimized configurations. It maintains consistent O--D counting without requiring manual intervention during the evaluated transition sequence.

The work’s significance lies in bridging the gap between laboratory benchmarks and operational reality. While many prior studies rely on controlled datasets or short capture scenarios, this thesis evaluates performance under practical constraints such as privacy masking and non-stationary lighting. This provides a more deployment-oriented basis for autonomous traffic monitoring using existing infrastructure.

Across analyses, the main performance driver is \emph{detection quality}. When a sufficiently accurate detector is used, differences between tracking algorithms become comparatively small in terms of aggregate counting. Tracker tuning mainly affects how errors are distributed across routes and rare classes. The experiments also show that the most challenging failure modes are practical rather than purely algorithmic. These include dense traffic occlusions, abrupt appearance changes introduced by privacy masking, and degraded visual cues under low-light conditions. Targeted post-processing for fragmented identities can mitigate some of these issues. However, it does not eliminate them.

The practical implication is that vision-based monitoring can provide high-frequency estimates of volumes, vehicle composition, and turning movements using existing infrastructure. This requires that the video stream meets basic quality requirements. In operational use, the main prerequisites are a stable camera view (no unexpected zoom or viewpoint changes) and sufficient temporal resolution for identity tracking. Privacy masking must also preserve enough vehicle structure for consistent association. Deployment also requires site-specific calibration of gate zones. Routine monitoring is needed to ensure that outputs remain within acceptable error bounds for the intended application.

At the same time, the results have clear boundaries. The tracking evaluation is based on one intersection. Cross-site generalization was not tested with a strict held-out site protocol. The dataset contains limited vulnerable road-user instances. This makes class-wise performance estimates for these categories statistically uncertain. Finally, the tracking ground truth is based on manual counting. It therefore includes some degree of human error, particularly in long, dense sequences.

Future work should focus on three areas. First, held-out-site evaluation to quantify generalization. Second, richer and more seasonally diverse data, especially for vulnerable road users. Third, improved robustness to deployment constraints such as low temporal resolution, privacy masking artifacts, occlusions, and challenging nighttime conditions. Within these boundaries, the proposed pipeline offers a scalable and lower-cost complement to existing traffic monitoring practices and supports the broader objective of more data-driven traffic engineering.
