
\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis demonstrates that YOLO-based vision methods can reliably extract traffic parameters from intersection video, including fine-grained vehicle classification and turning movements across both day and night conditions. Rather than requiring manual calibration at lighting transitions, an adaptive dual-tracker system automatically switches between optimized configurations, enabling continuous operation without intervention. More broadly, the work shows that detection quality is the primary performance driver; once a sufficiently accurate detector is deployed, different tracking algorithms yield comparable results. These findings confirm that vision-based monitoring is technically feasible for real-world traffic engineering applications, answering affirmatively all three research questions.

However, the study reveals critical constraints that temper this optimism. Performance depends on infrastructure that meets basic requirements—particularly adequate frame rate ($\geq$10 FPS) and visual continuity, which cannot be assured across all existing camera networks. The pipeline was optimized for motorized vehicles; vulnerable road users remain unreliable due to both data scarcity and fundamental visual limitations at typical camera-to-object distances. Cross-site generalization was not evaluated, meaning that conclusions about transferability to new intersections remain uncertain. These limitations are not peripheral; they fundamentally constrain the applicability and generalizability of the method.

The work's significance lies in bridging the gap between laboratory benchmarks and operational reality. Most prior vehicle-detection studies evaluate controlled datasets or short-term capture scenarios; this thesis validates performance across realistic seasonal transitions, privacy masking and dense traffic. The adaptive lighting mechanism is a pragmatic contribution that makes continuous deployment possible. Equally important, the open identification of constraints (frame rate thresholds, masking sensitivity, vulnerable-user gaps) provides a realistic foundation for practitioners deciding whether deployment is viable at a specific site, rather than overstating capability.

The path forward requires acknowledging that incremental improvements to vision-based methods alone will not overcome fundamental limitations. Future work should focus on the systematic characterization of frame rate–performance, the expanded collection of vulnerable road users, and cross-site validation to assess generalization. Vision-based monitoring will remain valuable for high frequency volume and movement monitoring where modest accuracy trade-offs are acceptable, but this thesis suggests it is not a universal replacement for existing infrastructure. Within its demonstrated scope, adequately equipped stations, motorized vehicle monitoring, and non-critical applications, the pipeline offers a scalable, low-cost tool for modernizing traffic data collection in line with Vision Zero objectives.
